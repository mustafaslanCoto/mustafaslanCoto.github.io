The challenge: why do some patients stay longer?

Hospitals need to understand why some patients stay longer than expected. Yet the true, isolated impact of specific diagnoses on Length of Stay (LoS) remains largely unknown, for a few reasons:

  • Tangled hospital data. Raw records mix together clinical diagnoses, patient age, chronic illnesses and ward congestion.
  • Correlations vs. causes. Standard machine learning predicts correlations, not causes. It cannot tell whether a diagnosis actively causes a delay or simply reflects why a patient arrived at the hospital in the first place.
  • Reclaiming bed capacity. Identifying clear, diagnosis-specific discharge protocols early in the admission is essential for taking proactive action, preventing avoidable delays and freeing up acute hospital beds.

Why is it so important?

Advancing NHS Wales’ Six Goals for Urgent Care

  • Goal 4 (Rapid Assessment & Cohorting): frontloading diagnostic clarification during the critical first 48 hours of admission.
  • Goal 5 (Optimal Hospital Care & Timely Discharge): activating diagnosis-tailored discharge readiness protocols before discharge.
  • Goal 6 (Home First / Step-Down): early social care and pharmacy coordination, avoiding costly hospital deconditioning.

Tailoring discharge to clinical need

  • Diagnosis-tailored discharge bundles: shifting ward practice from one-size-fits-all planning to targeted, multidisciplinary pathways aligned with the patient’s primary diagnosis.
  • First 48-hour protocol: frontloading diagnostic clarification early in the admission window to resolve clinical ambiguity before avoidable discharge delays build up.

Methodology

Assumed causal structure

We assume that a set of observed patient and admission characteristics \(X\) (the confounders) influence both which diagnosis \(D\) a patient receives and how long they stay (\(LoS\)). The diagnosis itself also has a direct effect on \(LoS\) — that is the effect we want to isolate.

Confounders

Double Machine Learning (DML)

For each diagnosis, let \(D=1\) indicate its presence and \(D=0\) its absence. Let \(LoS\) denote Length of Stay, and \(X\) the observed confounders.

The average predictive effect, \(\theta_0\), compares the expected \(LoS\) with the diagnosis present versus absent, averaged over patients’ observed characteristics:

\[ \theta_0 = \mathbb{E}\!\left[g_0(1,X)-g_0(0,X)\right]. \]

The outcome and diagnosis are modelled using the following regression equations:

\[ \begin{aligned} LoS &= g_0(D,X) + \varepsilon, &\quad \mathbb{E}[\varepsilon \mid X,D] &= 0, \\ D &= m_0(X) + \widetilde{D}, &\quad \mathbb{E}[\widetilde{D} \mid X] &= 0. \end{aligned} \]

Here, \(g_0(D,X)\) is the regression function for \(LoS\), while \(m_0(X)\) is the classification function for the diagnosis given the confounders.

Building on these two models, we construct an efficient estimator of the average predictive effect using the orthogonal score

\[ \theta_0 = \mathbb{E}\!\left[\varphi_0(W)\right], \qquad W=(LoS,D,X), \]

where

\[ \varphi_0(W) = g_0(1,X)-g_0(0,X) + \bigl(LoS-g_0(D,X)\bigr)H_0, \qquad H_0 = \frac{\mathbf{1}(D=1)}{m_0(X)} - \frac{\mathbf{1}(D=0)}{1-m_0(X)}. \]

Here, \(\mathbf{1}(\cdot)\) is an indicator function and \(H_0\) is the Horvitz–Thompson transformation. We repeat this for every diagnosis group to obtain diagnosis-specific average effects on LoS.

These are adjusted population-average effects, not individual patient-level causal effects. A causal interpretation relies on adequate confounder adjustment and on overlap between the diagnosis groups.

Key results

Causal effect sizes of diagnosis on LoS

Each point is the estimated effect (in days) of a diagnosis group on LoS, with its 95% confidence interval. Point size reflects how common the diagnosis is. Hover over a point for details.

The standout result is the uncoded group — admissions without a recorded diagnosis code. Diagnostic ambiguity is associated with roughly +2.5 extra days in hospital and affects around 28% of all admissions, which makes it one of the largest levers for reclaiming bed capacity.

Next steps

  • Co-designing discharge protocols & teams: collaborating directly with CVUHB clinicians and managers to determine how specialised discharge teams should be structured, and to establish tailored protocols targeting the delay-inducing diagnoses identified by our causal model.
  • Interactive decision-support tool: designing and deploying an interactive web application for clinicians and operational managers to prioritise high-impact diagnoses and measure the real-time bed-capacity impact of tailored discharge protocols.
  • Ward-level pilot implementation: running a prospective pilot study within priority acute wards to test whether early diagnostic clarification and proactive multidisciplinary reviews translate into measured reductions in Length of Stay.
  • Academic & practitioner dissemination: presenting findings at leading Operations Research venues (e.g., EJOR, POMS, INFORMS) and NHS executive seminars to gather feedback and evaluate whether the framework generalises across other NHS trusts and hospital settings.
